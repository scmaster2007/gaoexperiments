### "Let’s agree to agree: neural networks share classification order on real datasets":

I thought that this paper was insightful in that it implies there exists a real underlying structure of a dataset that optimizers (through either SGD / adaptive variants) take advantage of. TLDR: This paper offers a link between the training and test datasets, in that the algorithm learns data in the dataset in a remarkably similar order -> and, this layout / order of data is predictable. Both weaker and stronger models start by learning the same core examples, while powerful models are able to learn "harder" ones. The measurement for "hardness" is interesting, however, as the researchers measured complexity by a custom "accessibility score" scalar, which can be thought of intuitively as the expected value of number of correct classifications between the truth value and the learner's function. 

Follow up questions: Perhaps we could test out some alternative measures to "complexity" and see if this same phenomenon persists: a few ideas that are popping up are bit length, amount of variance in an image / question, kolgomorov complexity, etc... If the same phenomenon is observed, then that implies that the notion of complexity can be simplified further than simply the accuracy of the model on the task, which presupposes an innate "language" of complexity and structure in a dataset that can be measured. 

### On the implicit bias in deep-learning algorithms

This paper shows that models are inclined to look for "simpler" solutions in the search space. What this looks like concretely is that there are differing meanings to complexity regarding the complexity of the network itself. For a concrete example, simple overparametrized linear networks mathematically converge to the exact global minimum that is closest to the initialization point of w(0) in L2 distance, despite there being infinitely many local minima. Additionally, they also cover deep RELU nets, but they characterize that the implicit bias of RELU nets cannot be captured reliably by a single norm. Of course, this is the problem I would like to work on, finding some "measure" (or proving lack thereof) of feature-rich networks at scale. 

Follow up questions: How does the notion of sharpness relate to the possibility of norms in this space? it is my understanding that sharpness as a metric for generalization is flawed, does there exist other methods/metrics that are compatible with "lowest" complexity solutions? When we run adaptive optimizers like Adam/AdamW, what happens to the weight space and how does the linear regression norm fit, is it simply dependent on the landscape or also the optimizer itself? Let us go a bit deeper into the intuition/questioning here. For ReLU and other non-linear activations, I think of their effect as "bending" the geometry of the update/weight space. Can this bending be measured, and can it relateto solving implicit bias in deep neural nets? I think that focusing on this question of what function a network picks (despite there being infinitely many) specifically in adaptive optimizer settings is most valuable and interesting to me right now. 

### SGD learning on neural networks: leap complexity and saddle-to-saddle dynamics

This paper was interesting to read and very techincal. I will do a TLDR: essentially, instead of attempting to find a universal metric to "measure" functional complexity, they took ideas out of the field of computational complexity to determine the "hierarchy" of learned functions. What they found was that complex concepts can be made out of building blocks of smaller concepts that must be learned before learning the complex ones: specifically, this transition from lower stage to higher stage concepts is sudden: by getting stuck in local minima, it is able to extract all of the simplicity features from that point and then "jump" towards more complex solutions, repeating this process again. Thus, it directly answers the question of why neural networks trained by SGD are biased towards simpler solutions. 

Follow up questions: Has anyone done this with adaptive optimizers? (post-lit review answer: not really.) Does this work for all forms of data, even synthetic data? Perhaps synthetic data has traces of encoded data that allow for some weird feedback loop dynamics to occur which serve as a counterexample to leap complexity? 

### How deep neural networks learn compositional data: the random hierarchy model

TLDR: This paper introduces a new framework for the underlying structure of data, with their premise being that data is innately organized in a "tree" like structure, where the classes consisting of higher level features can be decomposed into subnodes of equivariant subfeatures across various layers in the tree. Apparently, neural networks are successful in leveraging this natural structure of the data because they leverage the fact that the equivariant subfeatures are interchangeable, which allows for more efficient and interesting computation (i.e. interesting solutions). 

To be honest, I am not as convinced on this as I am about the saddle-to-saddle paper, so I think I will stray away from studying datasets for now. But I agree that there is a necessity for a shared formalism of what data is / what it functions as.
